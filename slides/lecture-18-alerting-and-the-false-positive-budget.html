Read the Rule Name Before You React

  1. 1The rule matched a JA3 hash, which is a fingerprint of how a client says hello in TLS.
  2. 2It says this client software resembles software seen alongside adware. It does not say the traffic is adware.
  3. 3action: allowed means nothing was blocked. Suricata was watching, not enforcing.
Watch outThe gap between what a rule matched and what its name implies is where most bad incident reports come from. Cite the signature id, then say in your own words what was matched.

The Budget, Written Down

  • An everyday version. A restaurant with twenty tables does not take a hundred bookings and hope. It decides how many it can serve, and turns the rest away on purpose.
  1. 1Count the analyst hours you actually have per day.
  2. 2Divide by the minutes one alert takes to triage. That is your ceiling.
  3. 3Any rule that pushes you over the ceiling has to earn its place by catching something the others do not.
DefinitionFalse-positive budget. A stated number of tolerable false alarms per day, used to decide which rules run, rather than deciding after the queue becomes unbearable.

Three Ways to Get Under the Ceiling

  1. 1Tune the rule: narrow it, or disable it on the hosts where it is always wrong.
  2. 2Aggregate: alert on a count over a window, not on every match. Here that means one alert per source address per rule.
  3. 3Filter on severity: work only what the rule authors ranked highest.
Watch outThe fourth way, ignoring the queue, is what actually happens when nobody chooses one of the three. Choosing badly on purpose beats drifting there.

Decide: Combine Them, and Say What You Gave Up

  1. 1The threshold, written down: severity 1 only, aggregated to one alert per source and rule, and the two JA3 rules off. That is the only combination that could reach 50 a day.
  2. 2What you gave up: the two JA3 rules would have been the ones to catch a client fingerprint you care about. Write that down beside the rule.
  3. 3What to re-measure: the same counts after a week of real traffic, because a five-minute capture is a starting point and not a policy.

From Match to Alert in Kibana

  • Elasticsearch fires alerts from queries, so a threshold rule is a saved query plus a number plus a schedule.
  1. 1Query: which documents count, for example severity 1 alerts on this index.
  2. 2Threshold: how many, over what window, before anything happens.
  3. 3Action: what to do, from writing to an index to sending mail.
NoteKibana alerting is under Stack Management, and it needs the trial or a paid license. On the free license the cluster still stores and searches everything; it just will not run the rules for you.

What Your Rule Will Miss, Stated Up Front

  1. 1Anything below the threshold, which is the definition of the threshold.
  2. 2Anything slower than the window, because an attacker with patience is invisible to a five-minute count.
  3. 3Anything the sensor never recorded, which is Stage 1 and Stage 2 arriving as a consequence.
TipWrite the misses down beside the rule. A rule with its blind spots documented is a control. A rule without them is a comfort.

Triage, in Order

  1. 1Severity first, because someone already ranked them.
  2. 2Then category, because it says what kind of claim is being made.
  3. 3Then novelty: a signature you have never seen deserves a look before one you dismiss daily.
  4. 4Then correlation: does anything else in your data agree with this alert?

The Loop, on Your Alerting

  1. 1Analysis: the counts by severity, category and signature, and the cost arithmetic.
  2. 2Issue: the rule that dominates your queue, or the threshold nobody chose on purpose.
  3. 3Tools: the query, the window, the suppression.
  4. 4Solution: the budget you set and the rule you would disable first, with the reason.
