Three Things a Clock Changes

  1. 1You may not shuffle. Shuffling mixes the future into the past, and the past is all a detector ever has.
  2. 2You must split by time. Train on earlier, test on later, because that is the only split production resembles.
  3. 3A window becomes a feature. How many times in the last minute is a question a single row cannot answer.
DefinitionWindow feature. A number computed over the rows within some period before the current one, such as connections from this source in the last minute.

Three Ways Time Leaks Into a Model

  • Each one produces a score that is better than the truth.

The Three, Named

  1. 1Shuffled split. The same period appears in training and in test, so the model has seen the future.
  2. 2Feature computed over everything. A mean, a scaler, a vocabulary fitted across the whole file, which is the leak from Stage 4 wearing a clock.
  3. 3A label that arrives late. A column that is only known after the event you are predicting, which is the difficulty column from Lecture 4 in a new outfit.
Watch outAll three produce the same symptom: a validation score you cannot reproduce in production. That is why the fix is procedural rather than clever.

When the Expected Effect Does Not Appear

  1. 1Say so. An experiment that came out the other way is a result, not a mistake to bury.
  2. 2Then explain it: the bundle is five minutes of ordinary browsing, so the later minutes look like the earlier ones.
  3. 3Leakage needs something to leak. With no drift between periods and few repeated flows, shuffling costs nothing here.
TipThis is the whole method of the course in one slide. Run it, report what happened, explain it, and say what would have to be different for the other answer.

Where It Does Appear

  • CIC-IDS2017 has five days and different attacks on each of them. That is drift you can measure.
  1. 1Train on Tuesday's brute force and test on Thursday's web attacks: the model meets an attack it has never seen.
  2. 2Shuffle those days together and the same model looks far better, because Thursday is in its training data.
  3. 3The gap between those two numbers is the leak, and now it is large enough to see.

Splitting by Time, Correctly

  1. 1Sort by the timestamp before anything else.
  2. 2Choose the cut as a moment, not as a row count, so a busy minute does not straddle it.
  3. 3Fit every scaler, encoder and vocabulary on the training side of that moment only.
  4. 4Write the cut down in your report, because it is part of the result.
Watch outCross-validation with random folds is the shuffled split wearing a lab coat. On time-ordered data, use folds that move forward in time, each training on everything before the fold.

What a Window Cannot See

  1. 1Anything slower than the window. A scan spread over an hour is invisible to a one-minute count.
  2. 2Anything that happened before your data starts, which is the boundary problem from Stage 2.
  3. 3Anything a sensor did not record, which is Stage 1 arriving again.
NoteEvery window is a statement about the timescale of the attacks you expect. Write that statement down, because your successor will otherwise assume you measured it.
