One Neuron

  1. 1Multiply each input by a weight, add them up, add one more number called the bias.
  2. 2Pass that total through a switch that decides how much of it goes on.
  3. 3That is the whole neuron. Everything else is arrangement.
DefinitionActivation function. The switch. Without it, stacking layers is pointless: a sum of sums is still a sum, and the network could only draw straight lines.

Which Switch, and When

  1. 1ReLU in hidden layers, because it is cheap and it does not flatten out for large inputs.
  2. 2Sigmoid at the output when you want a probability between 0 and 1.
  3. 3Tanh when you want a value centred on zero, which matters inside sequence models.
NoteThe book derives these. You do not need the derivation to choose correctly, and the choice is what you will actually make.

Training, in Four Words

  1. 1Guess: run the inputs forward through the layers and get an answer.
  2. 2Measure: compare the answer with the label, as one number called the loss.
  3. 3Adjust: change every weight a little, in the direction that lowers the loss.
  4. 4Repeat: thousands of times. That is all training is.
AnalogyThe same loop you ran by hand when you moved the threshold in the evaluation session, except the machine moves 3,969 numbers instead of one, and it does it thirty times over the whole file.

What a Sequence Model Adds

  1. 1It reads rows in order and carries a small summary of what it has seen so far.
  2. 2That summary is updated at every row, and it is what the next prediction can use.
  3. 3So "this connection, from a host that made forty connections in the last minute" becomes expressible without you engineering the feature.
DefinitionLSTM. A sequence model with a memory it can choose to keep, update or forget, which is what lets it hold on to something from many rows back.
CIS 3370 Intrusion Detection Systems
12

Why Not Just Engineer the Feature

  1. 1You did engineer it, in the time-series session, and it worked.
  2. 2A hand-built window says "count in the last sixty seconds". You had to choose sixty.
  3. 3A sequence model learns what to remember and for how long, which is the trade: less choosing, more data needed and much less explainable.
TipBring the hand-built window feature along anyway. A sequence model with good features beats one that has to discover them, and you already know how to build them.

What You Feed It

  1. 1Not rows: windows. Each training example is a block of consecutive rows in order.
  2. 2The label is usually the label of the last row, or whether an attack occurred anywhere in the window.
  3. 3Every rule from the time-series session still applies, especially splitting by time before you make the windows.
Watch outBuild windows first and split second, and rows from the same window land on both sides of your split. That is the shuffled split from Slide 11 of the time-series session, wearing a different hat.

What To Report About a Network

  1. 1The architecture, in one line: layers, sizes, activation.
  2. 2The number of parameters, because it is the honest measure of what you asked the data to support.
  3. 3How many passes over the data, and whether the loss was still falling when you stopped.
  4. 4The comparison with your simplest model, always.
Why it mattersA network reported without its baseline is unreadable. Three points better than a tree is a result; 0.835 on its own is a number.
